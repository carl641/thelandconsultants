Land Surveying Supports Better Property Decisions

Land surveying establishes, measures, and documents important information about a property. Depending on the type of survey, that information may include boundary lines and corners, elevations, existing improvements, easements, rights-of-way, terrain features, or locations needed for construction.

The right survey depends on the project. Someone preparing to install improvements near a property line may need different information than a commercial buyer reviewing a property before closing. Likewise, an engineer designing a development needs different field data than a contractor preparing to lay out a building.

Working with a qualified land surveyor helps determine which surveying service fits the intended use of the property. Instead of treating every site the same, the survey scope can be developed around the property, available records, terrain, proposed work, and required deliverables.

Boundary and Property Line Surveys

A boundary survey formally defines the limits of a parcel and focuses on identifying its property corners and boundary lines. This type of work is commonly useful before buying, splitting, improving, or building on land.

A property line survey can help an owner understand where the surveyed boundaries are located before planning improvements such as fences, buildings, additions, or other work close to the edge of a parcel.

Boundary surveying involves more than measuring between visible markers. Surveyors may need to review deeds, plats, existing records, monuments, and field evidence before evaluating the property’s boundaries. The complexity can vary considerably from one parcel to another.

For property owners, establishing the boundary before construction can provide better information for planning and reduce the risk of relying on assumptions about where a line is located. If a fence is what prompted the question, our guide to fence and property line surveys in Madison County covers that scenario in more detail.

ALTA/NSPS Land Title Surveys

Commercial real estate transactions may call for an ALTA/NSPS Land Title Survey. These surveys are prepared according to established ALTA/NSPS Minimum Standard Detail Requirements and are often coordinated with title information and transaction requirements.

An ALTA/NSPS survey can document property boundaries along with relevant improvements, easements, rights-of-way, and other items within the agreed survey scope. Lenders, title professionals, attorneys, buyers, sellers, and developers may use the resulting information during commercial real estate due diligence.

Because the requirements of a transaction can differ, it is important to establish the requested scope and provide relevant title materials early in the process. Our article on the 2026 ALTA/NSPS standards walks through what changed and how to order this survey type correctly the first time.

Topographic Surveys for Planning and Design

A topographic survey documents elevations and the physical characteristics of a site. The resulting information may show contour lines as well as existing features such as buildings, fences, vegetation, drainage features, and other visible improvements relevant to the project.

This information can support architects, engineers, developers, and property owners during site planning and design.

Topographic data is particularly useful when a project involves grading, drainage, roads, parking areas, infrastructure, or new construction. Instead of designing from assumptions about the land, the project team can work from surveyed information about existing site conditions.

The Land Consultants uses a combination of conventional surveying equipment and technologies such as GPS/GNSS, robotic total stations, LiDAR, and photogrammetry depending on project requirements.

Drone/UAV LiDAR and Photogrammetry

Some sites benefit from aerial data collection, particularly when the property is large, complex, or difficult to access efficiently from the ground.

Our drone and UAV surveying services use LiDAR and photogrammetry for applications that may include high-resolution orthomosaic imagery, 3D point clouds, terrain models, volumetric calculations, and construction progress documentation.

LiDAR and photogrammetry are not interchangeable technologies. Photogrammetry develops measurements and models from overlapping imagery, while LiDAR uses laser measurements to collect three-dimensional data. The appropriate approach depends on site conditions and the required deliverables.

A professional surveyor can determine how aerial and ground-based methods should be combined for a particular project rather than assuming that a drone alone provides everything a survey requires.

Construction Layout and Staking

Once a project moves from design to construction, survey information must often be transferred accurately from the plans to the physical site.

Construction layout and staking can establish reference points for buildings, foundations, roads, utilities, grades, slopes, and other proposed improvements. Depending on the project, surveyors may return during multiple phases as work progresses.

Construction staking connects design information with field construction. Clear coordination among the surveyor, contractor, engineer, and other project professionals is important because the required points and elevations depend on the approved plans and the stage of construction.

Choosing a Property Surveyor for the Right Scope

Finding a property surveyor should involve more than asking whether someone can visit the site and take measurements. The more useful question is whether the surveying team can provide the particular service and deliverables your project requires.

Start by explaining why you need the survey. Are you purchasing property? Planning a fence or building? Dividing land? Designing a development? Preparing for commercial due diligence? Starting construction?

That purpose helps determine the appropriate survey type.

It also helps to provide available documents at the beginning. Deeds, plats, title commitments, design plans, previous surveys, and other property records can give the surveyor useful context.

When comparing survey companies, consider professional licensing, relevant capabilities, the proposed scope, technology appropriate to the project, and the deliverables you will receive. A low quote for a limited scope may not answer the questions that prompted you to order a survey in the first place.

From Initial Research to Final Survey Deliverables

A professional surveying project generally involves several stages rather than a single site visit.

Research and fieldwork may overlap depending on the project, but the scope set in the first conversation shapes every stage that follows.

The process begins with a conversation about the property and intended use of the survey. Understanding the project objective helps establish an appropriate scope.

Research follows. Depending on the project, surveyors may review deeds, plats, records, title materials, and other available documents before or alongside fieldwork.

During field data collection, the surveying team gathers the measurements and site information required by the scope. The equipment used can vary according to the property and survey type.

The collected information is then processed and evaluated. Final deliverables may include a survey plat, drawing, report, digital data, or other project-specific materials.

When an Existing Survey May Need to Be Updated

Having a previous survey can be helpful, but it does not always mean that new surveying work is unnecessary. A property’s conditions, improvements, ownership records, or project requirements may have changed since the earlier survey was completed.

For example, new buildings, fences, utilities, grading, access improvements, or other site changes may not appear on an older drawing. A lender, title company, engineer, developer, or other project professional may also require information prepared for the current transaction or scope of work.

An existing survey can still provide useful background information, so it is worth sharing with your property surveyor when discussing the project. The surveyor can review the available documents, understand what has changed, and determine whether additional research, field measurements, or updated deliverables are appropriate.

Rather than assuming an older survey will meet every current need, it is better to confirm that the available information matches the purpose of the project.

A Clearer Path to Your Next Property Decision

Land surveying provides property owners and project professionals with measured information that can support decisions about property, design, transactions, and construction. Keeping a few practical points in mind can help you prepare for your next surveying project.

  • Match the survey to the project. Boundary, ALTA/NSPS, topographic, drone/UAV, and construction surveys serve different purposes and provide different types of information.
  • Start with the purpose of the work. Explaining what you plan to buy, build, divide, design, or evaluate can help determine the appropriate surveying scope.
  • Provide relevant documents early. Deeds, plats, title information, previous surveys, and available design documents may provide useful information during the surveying process.
  • Consider the full scope of professional surveying. Depending on the project, surveying may involve property research, field measurements, analysis, mapping, and specialized technology.

Start with the decision, not the deliverable

The most useful first sentence in a survey conversation is not “I need a survey” — it is what you are trying to decide. Buying, building, dividing, designing, or closing each points toward a different scope, and naming it up front is what keeps the survey aligned with the project.
